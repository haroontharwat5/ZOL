# Recent, verified citations for the overtime paper

Every reference listed here has been checked against PubMed, publisher site, or the issuing body. Title, authors, journal, year, and a working link appear with each entry. Relevance is stated for the specific argument we are making.

Use these alongside the existing refs in `02_existing_overtime_sources.md` and the older staff-harm and patient-harm refs in `03_new_literature.md`.

---

## Why recency matters here

BMJ Quality & Safety reviewers look for two things on citations. First, that the claim is supported by work from the last three to five years where that work exists. Cortegiani 2020 and Saager 2014 are still valid as anchor citations, but if a newer study replicates them in a larger or cleaner dataset, the paper is stronger for citing both. Second, that we acknowledge mixed evidence rather than quoting the most favourable result. That is why the list below includes one reference, Guerra-Londono 2025, that actually undercuts the handover-harm claim. Citing it shows we read the field rather than the headlines.

## Verified recent references

### Patient-harm side

**Oh T-K, Jo Y-Y, Song I-A. Outcomes of after-hours surgeries performed under general anaesthesia: a South Korean nationwide cohort study. *Anaesthesia*, published online 10 February 2025. DOI: 10.1111/anae.16559.**
Link: https://associationofanaesthetists-publications.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/anae.16559
Design: national health-insurance cohort, 1.4 million patients screened, 1:5 propensity-score matched (57,497 after-hours vs 224,220 in-hours).
Why it matters for us: this is the newest large replication of the Cortegiani 2020 meta-analysis. Pairs well: we cite Cortegiani for the pooled estimate and Oh 2025 for the most recent single-country validation. Our Introduction claim that "after-hours surgery has a measurable mortality signal" now rests on a 2020 meta-analysis and a 2025 cohort of a quarter-million matched patients. That is the depth Q1 reviewers expect.

**Sakurai T. Assessing the influence of after-hours surgery: concerns with the confounders and conclusion. *Anaesthesia*, 2025. DOI: 10.1111/anae.16591.**
Link: https://associationofanaesthetists-publications.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/anae.16591
What it is: a correspondence item published in Anaesthesia responding to Oh 2025 with confounder concerns.
Why we cite it: citing the critique as well as the paper signals a balanced reading of the evidence. One sentence in the Discussion limitations ("the effect size remains debated; see Sakurai 2025") is enough.

**Guerra-Londono JJ, et al. The Impact of Intraoperative Anesthesiology Provider Handovers on Postoperative Complications After Hepatopancreatobiliary (HPB) Surgery. *Journal of Surgical Oncology*, 2025. PMID: 39388390. DOI: 10.1002/jso.27941.**
Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39388390/
Finding: no significant association between anesthesia team handovers and 90-day major complications in HPB surgery.
Why we cite it: the handover-harm claim (Saager 2014) is the mechanism that links cascaded overtime to patient outcomes. Guerra-Londono 2025 is a recent negative finding in complex cancer surgery. Citing it prevents a reviewer from writing "the authors ignored contradicting evidence." The correct framing is: early observational evidence showed a handover signal (Saager), subsequent trials and sub-specialty cohorts have produced mixed results (HandiCAP RCT; Guerra-Londono 2025), and the question is still open.

### Staff-harm / bridge side

**Health Services Safety Investigations Body (HSSIB). The impact of staff fatigue on patient safety. Investigation report, UK, 24 April 2025.**
Link: https://www.hssib.org.uk/patient-safety-investigations/the-impact-of-staff-fatigue-on-patient-safety/investigation-report/
What it is: a UK national safety-investigation body report. Surveyed doctors: 22% reported daily sleep deprivation, 19% weekly, 35% said tiredness had impaired their ability to treat patients.
Why it matters for us: this is the single most direct bridge reference for the paper's double-ended argument. It is a government-level patient-safety investigation that explicitly names staff fatigue as a patient-safety issue. Cite it in the Introduction and in the Discussion when we move from staff harm to patient harm. It is not peer-reviewed, but it is authoritative and very recent.

**Pittman P, Tiunn H-L, et al. Increased Utilization of Overtime and Agency Nurses and Patient Safety. *JAMA Network Open*, April 2025. PMID: 40172888.**
Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40172888/
Design: 70 US hospitals, Jan 2019 to Dec 2022. Ten AHRQ patient-safety indicators assessed.
Key finding as stated in the paper: exceeding breakpoint thresholds was associated with a 2.09% rise in pressure ulcers for overtime hours (and 6.44% for agency hours).
Important nuance: the paper is really about overtime *and* agency staffing, not overtime in isolation. The agency-staffing signal is bigger than the overtime signal. Cite it carefully. Recommended wording: "Overtime hours have also been directly linked to patient-safety indicators in a 70-hospital US cohort, independent of the larger effect seen for agency staffing." Do not present the 6.44% number as an overtime effect.

**Cho (Bae) J, Cho HJ, et al. Nurse Staffing, Work Hours, Mandatory Overtime, and Turnover in Acute Care Hospitals Affect Nurse Job Satisfaction, Intent to Leave, and Burnout: A Cross-Sectional Study. *International Journal of Public Health*, 2024;69:1607068. DOI: 10.3389/ijph.2024.1607068.**
Link: https://www.ssph-journal.org/journals/international-journal-of-public-health/articles/10.3389/ijph.2024.1607068/full
Design: cross-sectional, 397 nurses, 2022.
Key finding: mandatory overtime was significantly associated with intent to leave. Mandatory overtime was not a significant independent predictor of burnout in the multivariable model; workload and subjective health status were.
Why we cite it: this is the most recent direct overtime-to-intent-to-leave link. It replaces looser second-hand claims from older papers. It is also useful because it specifies that the overtime-burnout link is not always simple; workload matters more than hours. That nuance strengthens the paper rather than weakening it.

### Methods / measurement side

**Zhang J, Dunstan M, Pandit JJ. A tutorial on "capped utilisation" as a metric and key performance target in NHS England's Model Hospital operating theatres database. *Anesthesiology and Perioperative Science*, 2024. DOI: 10.1007/s44254-024-00073-3.**
Link: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44254-024-00073-3
Why we cite it: Methods defence. The paper argues that utilisation metrics hide real operational problems such as overtime when interpreted without context. We use it to justify choosing room-level overtime, weekday vs weekend split, and the staffing-pyramid framing rather than a single headline utilisation figure.

**Schouten AEM, et al. Operating Room Performance Optimization Metrics: a Systematic Review. *Journal of Medical Systems*, 2023;47:19. PMID: 36738376.**
Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36738376/
Why we cite it: systematic review of OR performance metrics, confirming that the field uses inconsistent definitions. Supports our decision to anchor the paper on Bauer 2020 definitions and on overtime as measured against a single shift-end clock. One-line Methods citation.

**MacMillan C, et al. What affects operating room turnover time? A systematic review and mapping of the evidence. *Surgery*, 2025. PMID: 40054053.**
Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40054053/
Why we cite it: turnover time is the classical OR-efficiency metric. Our data show idle time between cases has a median of 7 minutes and is not the bottleneck at Genk. Citing MacMillan 2025 shows that the field has now systematically reviewed this and that we are deliberately moving the focus from turnover time to overtime as the more informative metric. One-line Discussion citation.

### Not relevant anymore

**Korzhenevich A, Zander B. German OR benchmarking dataset. *Health Care Management Science* 27(3), 2024.**
This was useful for the cross-campus stratification story. Now dropped. Do not add to the BMJ QS version.

## Talking points for Maxim (the hour-long meeting)

### Why these five should go in

1. **HSSIB 2025** is the newest authoritative statement that staff fatigue is a patient-safety problem. For a double-ended argument paper, this is the bridge citation. It is from this year, it is from a national safety investigator, and it uses the same framing we are using.

2. **Pittman 2025** is the most direct quantification we have of the overtime-to-patient-safety link. 70 US hospitals, pressure ulcers and perioperative hemorrhage. The nuance is that the study actually looks at overtime plus agency staffing, and the agency signal is larger. We cite the overtime effect precisely and do not overstate.

3. **Oh 2025** is the largest post-Cortegiani after-hours cohort in the literature. 281,717 matched patients. Pairing Cortegiani 2020 with Oh 2025 gives the introduction two strong citations spanning meta-analysis to recent single-country replication.

4. **Cho 2024** is the newest peer-reviewed link between mandatory overtime and intent to leave, which is exactly the turnover claim we make on the staff-harm side.

5. **Zhang / Dunstan / Pandit 2024** is a methods-defence citation. It argues that utilisation metrics hide operational reality, which is why we chose room-level overtime instead. This is also a Pandit paper, which is useful because we already cite the Pandit 2012 FCOTS paper.

### Why I am also citing one paper that disagrees with us

Guerra-Londono 2025 found no association between anesthesia handover and complications in HPB surgery. The Saager 2014 finding was positive. Citing both protects us from a reviewer writing "the authors cherry-picked the supportive evidence." We present the handover claim as "observational evidence suggests elevated risk, with recent sub-specialty cohorts producing mixed results." That is the honest read of the field.

### What this adds to the current literature files

`02_existing_overtime_sources.md` and `03_new_literature.md` are the operational and safety backbone. They are not out of date, but they lean heavily on papers from 2008 to 2020. Adding these six recent references (Oh 2025, Pittman 2025, HSSIB 2025, Cho 2024, Zhang et al. 2024, Guerra-Londono 2025, plus Schouten 2023 and MacMillan 2025 as methods citations) makes every section of the paper defensible against the "where is the recent evidence" reviewer comment.

### What I am not adding

Korzhenevich 2024 is out. The cross-campus benchmarking story was dropped when we pivoted, and this paper only supported that story.
